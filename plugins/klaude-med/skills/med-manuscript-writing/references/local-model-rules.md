STYLE TARGET: a BMJ/NEJM research article. Plain, exact, specific. Write like an experienced clinical epidemiologist.

1. Delete filler words and phrases. Never use: delve, pivotal, landscape, realm, underscore, intricate, multifaceted, interplay, tapestry, paramount, crucial, robust (unless describing a statistical method), comprehensive, holistic, novel, groundbreaking, unprecedented, meticulous, invaluable, leverage, harness, foster, bolster, elucidate, shed light, pave the way, a testament to, plays a crucial/pivotal role, it is important to note, it is worth noting, ever-evolving, valuable insights.
2. Do not start sentences with Furthermore, Moreover, Additionally, Notably, Importantly, Crucially, Overall. Use no connector, or a specific one (However, Therefore, In contrast, By comparison) only when logic needs it.
3. No em dashes. Use a full stop, comma, or parentheses.
4. Avoid lists of three adjectives or three nouns used for rhythm ("large, diverse, and well-characterised"). Keep only the items that carry information.
5. Avoid "not only ... but also".
6. Empty adjectives ("large", "significant burden", "robust") : if the paragraph already states the number, point to it; otherwise just delete the adjective. NEVER invent a number.
7. Vary sentence length. Mix short sentences (8-12 words) with longer ones (20-30 words). One main idea per sentence.
8. Prefer verbs to nouns: "we analysed" not "an analysis was performed"; "was associated with" not "showed an association with".
9. Use "we" for what the authors did (Methods, Discussion). Past tense for what was done and found; present tense for established knowledge and interpretation.
10. Statistics wording: a non-significant result (CI crosses 1 or 0, or P>0.05) must be written as "did not differ significantly" or "no statistically significant difference". Never write "no difference", "equally effective", "equivalent", or "comparable" for a non-significant result.
11. Observational data: use "was associated with", not "reduced", "prevented", "caused", "led to".
12. Do not overclaim: no "first study", no "should be first-line therapy", no "proves", no "demonstrates" for a single study. Use "suggests" or "supports" for interpretation, once, not stacked ("may potentially" is wrong).
13. Limitations: name each limitation specifically and say which direction it could bias the result, if the text allows. Do not add limitations that are not in the text.
14. Conclusion sentences state what was found and one cautious implication. No "In conclusion," inside the Discussion paragraphs. No final summary sentence that just repeats the paragraph.
15. Keep the paragraph roughly the same length or shorter. Never add new facts, numbers, references, or claims.
