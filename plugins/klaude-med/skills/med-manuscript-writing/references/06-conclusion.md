# Conclusion — rules distilled from 14-paper corpus

## 1. Job of this section
Answer the stated question using the data and nothing beyond them, state one scoped implication, and stop. Applies to two places: the abstract Conclusions line and the main-text Conclusions paragraph (or the last Discussion paragraph when the journal has no separate heading) [B1,A4]. No new numbers, no new claims, no new citations except an ongoing trial or guideline already discussed [D3,A4].

## 2. Move structure by article type
| Type | Moves (one line each) |
|---|---|
| RCT, positive (NEJM-style) | (1) Population + intervention + measured outcomes only; (2) optional qualifier tied to an external threshold ("clinically meaningful") [B1]. Registration/funding sit in parentheses at the end of the abstract conclusion [B1]. |
| RCT, NS primary (BMJ-style) | (1) "Trial showed X is not superior to Y" (superiority frame, bounded to tested comparison, population, timepoint); (2) one-line research direction naming a target, not a command [B2]. |
| Observational cohort / population | (1) Policy or debate context in one clause (optional); (2) main association with direction, sites/subgroups; (3) conditional chain (replication, mechanism, exposure burden) BEFORE any strong noun such as "modifiable risk factor"; (4) link to existing guidance, attributed not self-recommended [B3,B4]. |
| Meta-analysis, pairwise | (1) Support/no-support for existing recommendation; (2) population-restricted benefits; (3) certainty in two tiers, each tier paired with its outcomes; (4) main uncertainty + the trial design that would resolve it; (5) named ongoing trial [C3]. |
| Meta-analysis, dose-response | (1) Headline association; (2) subtype/biomarker-consistent finding; (3) policy implication stated with design-appropriate verb; (4) generalisability caution placed adjacent to recommendation; (5) mechanism research [C1]. |
| Network MA | (1) Verdict scoped as "best available evidence", consistency with other evidence; (2) separate caveat paragraph listing the same limitations as the body; (3) next design (IPD-NMA) [C2]. |
| SR with MA (PRISMA) | (1) Scale statement; (2) main finding with its conditional ("when adjusted for confounders") repeated; (3) one forward-looking methodological recommendation [D1]. |
| SR without MA (SWiM) | (1) Evidence is limited despite volume; (2) no option shown superior; (3) the one supported conclusion; (4) unresolved question; (5) design requirement for future work [D2]. |
| Umbrella review | (1) Direction of risk; (2) strongest-evidence domains; (3) weak domains "warrant further study"; (4) research + policy action naming instruments [D3]. |

## 3. Length budget
- Abstract conclusion: 1–2 sentences, 25–60 words [B1,A4] (estimate).
- Main-text Conclusions: RCT 40–70 words; cohort 70–190; MA/SR 100–200; NMA/pairwise MA up to ~250 if certainty is graded (corpus ranges; estimates).
- Key-points box ("known / adds"): 3 + 3 one-sentence bullets, each under ~30 words [B2,D1,C2].

## 4. Template bank
**Answer sentence**
1. Our [design] showed that [intervention] [verb] [outcome] among [population].
2. Among [population with condition], [drug] reduced [outcome 1] and [outcome 2] and improved [PRO].
3. [Trial acronym] indicates that [strategy] is not superior to [comparator] for [outcome] at [timepoint].
4. In [population], [exposure] was associated with a higher risk of [outcome], particularly [subtype].
5. [Intervention] in [setting] was associated with lower [outcome] in [k] [stratifier] groups, and the gradient by [stratifier] persisted.
6. Pooled estimates indicate that [exposure] is associated with [outcome], [with / without] [qualifier, e.g. after confounder adjustment].

**Evidence-strength sentence**
7. Certainty was [high] for [outcome list] and [moderate] for [outcome list].
8. The strongest evidence concerned [outcome domains]; evidence on [domains] was [weak/limited] and warrants further study.
9. Current evidence does not allow [method] to be identified as superior for [outcome].

**Implication sentence (gated)**
10. If replicated in [setting] and supported by [mechanistic evidence], [exposure] could represent a modifiable risk factor for [outcome].
11. These findings are consistent with [existing guidance] and may support [policy option].
12. The results support [existing recommendation] in [restricted population].
13. These data can inform clinician–patient discussion of [decision].

**Caution sentence (place beside the implication)**
14. Generalisation to [population] needs caution because most included [studies] come from [setting].
15. Because [design feature], causal inference is not possible; [association language] is used throughout.
16. Estimates for [agent] should be read alongside [limitation list].

**Next-step sentence (specific, never "more research is needed")**
17. A [design] in [population] that [design feature] is needed to confirm whether [surrogate] changes lead to [clinical outcome].
18. The ongoing [trial name] ([registration ID]) will test [question] over [duration] in [N] participants.
19. Future primary studies should adjust for [named factor set].
20. Studies should compare [methods] using clearly defined [parameters].

## 5. Tense / voice / person
| Element | Tense | Voice/person |
|---|---|---|
| What the study did/found | Past | Impersonal (no "we") |
| Interpretation of the answer | Present | Active, hedge verb |
| Implication | Present with modal / conditional (could, might, would) | Impersonal |
| Future work | "should" / "are needed" / future for a named ongoing trial | Impersonal |
[A4,B2,C3,D2]

## 6. Claim-strength ladder (strongest → weakest)
1. "reduced / improved" (bare verb): only for randomised comparison with a significant primary result, limited to measured outcomes [B1].
2. "supports [existing recommendation]" / "strongly supports": only when the design and certainty back it; for observational pooled data prefer "supports" and name the limit [C1,C3].
3. "is associated with" (default for all observational and pooled-observational findings) [B3,C1,D1,D3].
4. "suggests" / "indicates": interpretation of a single association or trend [A4,B3].
5. "might / may / could" + named reason: mechanisms, policy effects, exploratory subgroups [C1,C3].
6. "not superior to": superiority trial with NS primary; never "equivalent" or "equally effective" unless an equivalence/non-inferiority margin was pre-specified [B2].
7. "no statistically significant difference / did not reach significance": any NS result. Never "no effect", "no difference", "no association" without the CI and a power caveat [B3,C1].
- A CI crossing the null is not a reduction/increase; do not write "reduces by X%".
- Causal verbs (reduces, causes, prevents) only for randomised contrasts, or after stating identifying assumptions [A3]. Observational design: keep "associated" in the conclusion even if the Discussion argues plausibility [B3].
- Conditional gate precedes the strong noun: "if replicated and mechanistically supported … would represent" [B3].
- Never go beyond: unmeasured outcomes, longer follow-up, other populations, dose ranges not tested, cost-effectiveness [B1,B2,C2].

## 7. Numbers and statistics
- Default: no numbers in Conclusions [B1,B2,B3,D3,A4]. Exception: a single headline quantity already given in Results/Abstract, repeated unchanged [C1].
- Never print a P value alone; if cited, carry estimate + CI [A4].
- For certainty, use tier labels (high/moderate/low/very low) or evidence class; give counts only in Results [C3,D3].
- NS HR/OR/RR never appear as headline figures in a conclusion or box.

## 8. Reporting-guideline checklist
- CONSORT: interpretation consistent with results, benefits and harms balanced; generalisability; registration and funding (abstract conclusion or end of text).
- STROBE: key results with reference to objectives; cautious overall interpretation; generalisability.
- PRISMA 2020: general interpretation, limitations of evidence and of the review process, implications for practice, policy, future research; registration.
- PRISMA-NMA / GRADE: certainty stated; ranking claims scoped by confidence.
- SWiM: state why pooling was not done and what that means for interpretation.

## 9. Do / Don't
- Do answer the question in the question's own key terms. Don't introduce new vocabulary or a new outcome.
- Do state the limit next to the implication. Don't bury caution in a separate distant paragraph.
- Do name a specific next design. Don't write a generic call for further research.
- Do report null results with the same plainness as positive ones. Don't dramatise ("disappointingly", "failed").
- Do keep funder/registration to a parenthesis. Don't add a call to action for clinicians the data cannot support.
- Do end on the research or reader task. Don't end on a slogan or restated abstract.

## 10. LLM-draft failure modes → fix
| Tell | Why it fails | Rewrite pattern |
|---|---|---|
| "In conclusion, this study sheds light on…" | Filler opener, no answer | Open with the answer sentence (template 1–6) |
| Restates every result with numbers | Duplicates Results; new-number risk | One sentence, direction only |
| "paves the way for future research" / "further research is needed" | Empty | Name design + population (17–20) |
| "first study to…" / "novel" | Unverifiable, overclaim | State the gap filled; drop priority claim |
| "X proves / demonstrates that X causes Y" in observational data | Causal overreach | "was associated with"; add conditional gate |
| NS result → "no difference" / "equally effective" | Wrong inference | "no statistically significant difference"; "not superior to" |
| Policy imperative ("must be implemented") | Beyond data, no mandate | "may support [policy option]", attributed to existing guidance |
| Closing slogan or triplet ("improve outcomes, reduce costs, enhance quality") | Unmeasured outcomes | List only measured outcomes |
| Hedge stack "may potentially suggest" | Vague | One hedge + named reason |
| Mini-summary after every paragraph, then another in the Conclusion | Repetitive | Delete; keep one conclusion |

## 11. Mini worked example (fictional)
**Before (LLM-style):** In conclusion, this groundbreaking study sheds light on the pivotal role of drug Z in managing condition Q, demonstrating that Z significantly improves outcomes and paving the way for transformative change. Z should be immediately adopted in clinical practice, and further research is needed.

**After (journal-style):** In this randomised trial of 312 adults with mild condition Q, drug Z did not significantly reduce symptom score at 12 weeks compared with placebo (mean difference 0.8 points, 95% CI -0.4 to 2.6; numbers shown only because this is an abstract-style conclusion with one headline estimate). The CI spans values below and above the pre-specified minimum important difference of 2 points, so the trial cannot exclude a modest benefit. A larger trial with 24-week follow-up in participants with moderate disease is needed.
